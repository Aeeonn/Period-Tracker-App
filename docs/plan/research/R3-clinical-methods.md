# R3 — Evidence-based computations and clinical references

## Decision-relevant summary

- **Documented; high confidence:** Calendar history alone cannot precisely locate ovulation. Wilcox, Bull and Fehring show substantial variation, including among regularly cycling participants. BBT, LH and mucus measure different signals; none supplies an individually calibrated conception probability.[1–9]
- **Documented; high:** Published next-period models explicitly distinguish missed tracking from physiology and quantify prediction uncertainty. Their cohort results do not establish performance for these two users, irregular-cycle conditions, or Flo parity.[5,6]
- **Documented; high:** ACOG Committee Opinion 700 remains published and is **reaffirmed 2025**. IVF dating uses embryo age and transfer date; clinician-established dating differs from a calendar estimate.[10]
- **Documented; high:** Bleeding references are not interchangeable: FIGO uses 24–38-day cycles and bleeding up to eight days; ACOG patient guidance uses 21–35 and up to seven. PCOS criteria use yet another purpose-specific definition. Preserve source, age and context rather than silently combining thresholds.[16–20]
- **Documented; high:** Current NICE NG88 was updated **7 July 2026**, deleting its ferritin-testing recommendation; NG23 was updated **15 April 2026**, including HRT bleeding advice. ESHRE's current endometriosis page presents 2022; its PCOS page links the 2023 international guideline.[13,17,20,21]
- **Documented/Reported; high/medium:** drip is GPL-3.0-or-later, but its `sympto` dependency is **AGPL-3.0-or-later**. MRS has historical research-use permission; application reproduction permissions are not fully established. The primary Marquette dataset has participant reuse consent but no explicit standardized license; its popular Kaggle mirror says **Unknown**. An uploader-labelled CC0 synthetic dataset exists, without demonstrated physiological validity.[14,24–26]

**Inferred; high:** These findings support evidence-linked planning, not contraception, diagnosis, “safe days,” guaranteed accuracy, or a Flo-equivalent probability curve. Algorithm selection and independent medical-safety review remain later work. No product choice requiring a captain answer arose in this factual investigation.

## 1. Next-period prediction, variability, outliers and missed logs

**D/H:** Bull 2019 examined **612,613 ovulatory cycles from 124,648 Natural Cycles users**, mean cycle 29.3 days, follicular phase 16.9 and luteal phase 12.4. The study excluded several conditions, recent hormonal contraception, pregnancies and inadequate temperature tracking; detected ovulation depended on the app's algorithm. It is not an unselected population or ground-truth validation of a new predictor.[3]

**D/H, abstract evidence:** Fehring 2006 followed 141 healthy, regularly cycling women, producing 1,060 usable cycles. Mean length was 28.9 days; 95% were 22–36. Follicular variation contributed most; only 25% of participants had all six estimated fertile days within days 10–17. Urinary monitor peak, not direct observation of egg release, estimated ovulation.[4]

**D/H:** Li et al., published online 2021/in JAMIA 2022, used a hierarchical generative model with latent typical length and tracking-skip probability. Observed intervals can comprise multiple unlogged cycles; predictions update conditional on the current elapsed day. It beat mean/median and neural-network baselines on its dataset, using the first ten cycles to predict the eleventh. Participants were 21–33 with natural cycles; filtering included missing period data beyond 90 days and user-excluded cycles.[5]

**D/H:** Its operational outlier definition—menses longer than ten days—was a study preprocessing choice, **not a clinical warning threshold**. Urteaga 2021 describes a flexible generative model accommodating under-dispersion and calibrated cycle-length uncertainty.[5,6]

**I/H:** Mean, median, adherence-aware and uncertainty-aware models are evidence-backed candidates, not selections. No retrieved clinical guideline specifies universal trimming, a minimum history, or a best next-period formula for every irregular cycle. A doubled interval does not prove a missed log; pregnancy, anovulation and genuine long cycles remain distinguishable possibilities. Backtesting must separate prediction error, interval coverage, tracking artifacts and cohort eligibility.

## 2. Ovulation and fertile-window evidence

| Signal/method | Evidence and limits |
|---|---|
| Calendar | **D/H:** ASRM describes presumed ovulation at cycle length minus 14, with the preceding five days plus presumed ovulation as an estimated window. It reports maximum exact-day accuracy of 21% in a 949-person app comparison. This documents a convention, not a fixed personal luteal phase.[7] |
| Historical rhythm / Standard Days | **D/H:** The WHO/JHU handbook describes shortest of the last six cycles minus 18 and longest minus 11; Standard Days uses days 8–19 for predominantly 26–32-day cycles. These are distinct named methods, not evidence of pregnancy impossibility elsewhere. Their contraceptive instructions are **not adopted for this project**.[8] |
| BBT | **D/H:** The handbook describes a waking-temperature rise of roughly 0.2–0.5°C after ovulation and sustained higher readings. Fever and measurement conditions matter. BBT is mainly retrospective evidence of a shift, not advance localization of all potentially fertile days.[8] |
| LH | **D/H:** ASRM says ovulation can occur anytime within two days after a urinary surge, with false positives in about 7% of cycles. A test is not proof that ovulation occurred; the surge does not capture the entire preceding window.[7] |
| Cervical mucus | **D/H:** Clear/slippery secretions associate with higher fecundability, but are not necessary for pregnancy. The handbook's “peak” is the last clear, slippery, stretchy/wet day, recognizable retrospectively. Vaginal infection and other secretion changes complicate interpretation.[7,8] |
| Symptothermal | **D/H:** The handbook combines mucus and temperature and waits for the later marker-based endpoint. Inspected `sympto@3.0.2` operationalizes six preceding temperature measurements, three higher measurements with a third ≥0.2°C above the low-temperature maximum, exception rules, and three lower-quality mucus observations following a peak. This is **documented code behavior**, not independent clinical validation or a universal clinical specification.[8,9] |

**I/H:** Missing, excluded or disturbed readings, rounding, observation timing, hormonal treatment, postpartum/breastfeeding and perimenopause require explicit method-specific handling. Do not splice rules from different methods or treat a detected shift as contraceptive clearance. Exact exception logic needs later specification and audit.

## 3. Conception likelihood: three different quantities

**D/H, abstract evidence:** Wilcox 1995 recruited 221 healthy women planning pregnancy; 625 cycles had estimated ovulation and 192 initiated pregnancies, detected with urinary hCG. Observed conceptions followed intercourse in a six-day interval ending on estimated ovulation. Estimated conception probability ranged from **0.10 at five days before to 0.33 on estimated ovulation day**.[1]

**D/H, abstract evidence:** Wilcox 2000 used 696 cycles. On each cycle day 6–21, at least 10% of participants were estimated to be **within their fertile window**. Only about 30% had that whole window within days 10–17. That 10% is not the chance that intercourse causes pregnancy.[2]

**D/H:** ASRM instead summarizes peak fecundability in the two days before ovulation and clinical-pregnancy estimates after a single intercourse act of 3.2% on cycle day 8, 9.4% on day 12 and less than 2% on day 21.[7]

**I/H:** Distinguish (a) membership in an estimated window, (b) pregnancy following intercourse at an ovulation-relative offset, and (c) pregnancy following intercourse on a calendar cycle day. These are cohort estimates with different endpoints and timing uncertainty. The differing peak descriptions must not be silently merged; the retrieved 1995 abstract does not resolve a complete common curve. Multiple intercourse acts are not independent trials to add or multiply casually. No retrieved source validates calibrated daily personal percentages for this app. Observed absence of conception outside a study window does not establish zero real-world risk.

## 4. Pregnancy/IVF dating and milestones

**D/H:** ACOG CO700: conventional EDD = first day of last menstrual period + **280 days**, assuming a 28-day cycle and day-14 ovulation. First-trimester crown–rump-length dating through 13+6 weeks is most accurate, approximately ±5–7 days. Redating thresholds depend on scan timing: before 9+0, discrepancy >5 days; 9+0–13+6, >7; 14+0–15+6, >7; 16+0–21+6, >10; 22+0–27+6, >14; first scan at ≥28+0, >21 with particular caution. No confirming/revising ultrasound before 22+0 is “suboptimally dated.” Established EDD changes should be rare and clinician-documented.[10]

**D/H:** IVF uses transfer date and embryo age: day-5 EDD = transfer +261 days; day-3 = +263.[10] **I/H, arithmetic generalization:** transfer + (266 − embryo age in days); gestational age on transfer day is 14 + embryo age. For a selected EDD, gestational days on a date = 280 − days until EDD. These arithmetic identities do not resolve uncertain dating inputs.

**D/H:** NICE NG201 milestones include booking by 10+0, dating scan 11+2–14+1, anomaly scan 18+0–20+6, and risk-indicated gestational-diabetes testing 24+0–28+0.[11] **I/H:** These are UK care schedules, not universal fetal-development guarantees; localized care and clinician-confirmed dates take precedence. Educational milestones should be written originally, not copied from Flo.

## 5. Perimenopause staging and symptom scores

**D/H:** STRAW+10 is the 2011 workshop's 2012 publication. Its abstract was accessible; original full text was not retrievable in this environment. **R/M:** The accessible 2022 nomenclature review reproduces early transition as persistent ≥7-day cycle-length variation recurring within ten cycles, and late transition as ≥60 days of amenorrhea. Menopause/final menstrual period is identified retrospectively after twelve months without menstruation.[12]

**D/H:** NICE NG23 clinically identifies perimenopause in otherwise healthy people ≥45 with new vasomotor symptoms and cycle changes; twelve-month amenorrhea identifies menopause when not using hormonal contraception. Hormonal treatment confounds identification. Under-40 suspected premature ovarian insufficiency requires clinical assessment and testing, not a symptom-derived stage; these users are 21.[13]

**D/H:** MRS has eleven items scored 0–4, three domain sums and a total **0–44**. It measures symptom burden/health-related quality of life, not menopause probability. The 2003 authors explicitly permit research use without formal permission; the 2004 article is CC BY 2.0.[14]

**D/H:** MENQOL's original 1996 development and Greene's validation/norm studies are documented.[15] **I/M, licensing finding:** Applicable app/electronic-administration permission for MENQOL and Greene was not established; ePROVIDE returned only a JavaScript shell. MRS's historical research permission and article license should not be expanded into a blanket grant for every questionnaire version or modified app presentation. Preserve validated wording/scoring only with established permission; original substitute questions cannot retain a validated-scale claim. No evidence here establishes a universal monthly “Perimenopause Score.”

## 6. Bleeding and cycle warning references

**D/H:** FIGO System 1 describes nongestational bleeding frequency, regularity, duration and perceived volume; System 2 classifies possible causes as PALM–COEIN. The 2018 revision has a published corrigendum; the 2023 explanatory publication retains these systems. Its six-month reference excludes pregnancy/puerperium: normal frequency 24–38 days, bleeding ≤8 consecutive days, shortest-to-longest variation ≤9 days at ages 18–25/42–45 or ≤7 at 26–41. These are descriptors, not diagnoses or emergency thresholds.[16]

**D/H:** ACOG's patient FAQ uses 21–35 days and ≤7 bleeding days; highlights between-period/post-sex bleeding, hourly product saturation, irregularity, and absent periods for 3–6 months. Its emergency example is changing products hourly for >2 hours **with** chest pain, breathlessness and lightheadedness/dizziness.[18] NHS heavy-period indicators include changes every 1–2 hours, clots >2.5 cm, flooding and interference with life.[19]

**D/H:** NICE NG88 emphasizes quality-of-life impact over estimated millilitres, history of pain/pressure/persistent intermenstrual bleeding, and full blood count. Current recommendation 1.2.8 is deleted; do not propagate its old “do not routinely test ferritin” wording as current guidance.[17]

**D/H:** PBAC review: validated thresholds ranged **50–185**, product/chart dependent. Scores are not universally millilitres; reproduced chart images carry separate publisher permissions.[23]

**D/H:** NHS ectopic-pregnancy guidance connects possible pregnancy, unusual bleeding, one-sided pain or shoulder-tip pain with medical assessment; sudden intense pain with dizziness/fainting can require emergency care even without a positive pregnancy test.[19] Its displayed review was overdue. **I/H:** These are source evidence for later warning design, not an exhaustive triage algorithm or safety certification.

## 7. Symptom-checker criteria

- **D/H — PCOS:** 2023 adult criteria require two of hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology (AMH can substitute for ultrasound in adults), after excluding alternatives. Irregular cycles plus hyperandrogenism can avoid ultrasound/AMH. ≥3 years after menarche: <21 or >35 days or <8 cycles/year; >90 days for any cycle more than a year after menarche warrants assessment. Acne alone is a weak predictor; normal-length cycles do not prove ovulation.[20]
- **D/H — Endometriosis:** ESHRE 2022 includes cyclical/noncyclical period pain, deep pain with intercourse, painful urination/defecation, fatigue and infertility, among other signs. Negative ultrasound/MRI does not exclude disease. Its panel notes potential diary usefulness but no evidence that an app shortens diagnosis time.[21]
- **D/H — Fibroids/HMB:** NICE's pain/pressure, pelvic-mass and bleeding patterns inform clinician investigation, including ultrasound or hysteroscopy. Logs cannot establish lesion presence, size or subtype; overlapping causes remain possible.[17]
- **D/H — PMS:** ACOG's public criteria describe symptoms in the five premenstrual days for three consecutive cycles, resolving within four days after onset and impairing activity; record symptoms daily for 2–3 months. CPG7 (December 2023) covers PMS/PMDD, but its detailed diagnostic text was gated. No executable PMDD symptom-count rule is asserted here.[22]

**I/H:** These references support explainable “consider discussing this pattern” information, not a diagnostic score, rule-based certainty or a validated Flo checker replica.
